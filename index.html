A no-shame guide

Cum, explained.

Almost every guy has questions about semen and ejaculation and almost nobody asks them. Here's what's normal, what isn't, and when it's worth seeing a doctor.

Made for a sex ed project. This is general health information, not medical advice. If something worries you, a doctor or clinic has heard it all before.

The words

Slang is fine. Knowing the medical words helps when you talk to a doctor or look something up.

Semen / cum
The fluid that comes out when you ejaculate. Sperm are only a small part of it.
Ejaculation / nutting, coming
The muscle contractions that push semen out, usually at orgasm.
Pre-ejaculate / precum
Clear fluid that comes out when you're aroused, before ejaculation.
Orgasm
The peak feeling. It usually happens with ejaculation, but the two can happen separately.
Nocturnal emission / wet dream
Ejaculating in your sleep. Most common in the teen years, and completely normal.
Refractory period
The recovery time after ejaculating before you can get hard or come again. It ranges from minutes to a day and tends to get longer with age.

What's actually in it

Most of semen comes from glands, not the testicles. Sperm are a few percent of the volume. The rest is fluid that feeds and protects them.

  • Seminal vesicles. Sugar (fructose) for energy and proteins that make it thick.~65–75%
  • Prostate. Enzymes that make it go watery again, plus zinc.~25–30%
  • Sperm from the testicles. Tens of millions per mL.~2–5%
  • Other glands. Mostly precum, which lubricates the urethra.<1%
1.5–5 mLTypical amount. A teaspoon is 5 mL.

Is this normal?

Semen changes with how often you ejaculate, how hydrated you are, and your age. Most variation is nothing. Here's how to tell.

  • White, gray, or cloudy

    Normal

    This is the usual color.

  • A little yellowish

    Normal

    Common after a long time without ejaculating, or if some urine mixes in.

  • Thick and clumpy, or thin and watery

    Normal

    It's thick at first and turns watery within half an hour. It's thinner if you ejaculate often.

  • Less than usual after coming a lot

    Normal

    Your body needs time to refill. Volume also drops slowly with age.

  • Wet dreams

    Normal

    Not a sign of anything. They often happen less once you ejaculate more regularly.

  • Sore or achy balls after arousal without finishing

    Normal

    Often called "blue balls." It's uncomfortable but harmless, and it goes away on its own. It's never a reason to pressure anyone into anything.

  • Finishing sooner than you want

    Keep an eye on it

    Extremely common, especially when you're young or nervous. Stop-start techniques and pelvic floor exercises help. If it keeps bothering you, a doctor can help and won't judge.

  • Taking a long time, or not being able to finish

    Keep an eye on it

    Stress, alcohol, and some medications (like certain antidepressants) can cause this. Bring it up with a doctor if it keeps happening.

  • Orgasm with little or nothing coming out

    Keep an eye on it

    Some medications, surgery, and diabetes can cause a "dry orgasm" or send semen backward into the bladder. Not dangerous, but worth mentioning to a doctor, especially if you want kids someday.

  • Pink, red, or brown

    See a doctor

    That's blood. In younger men it's often harmless and goes away on its own, but get it checked, especially if it keeps happening or you're over 40.

  • Green, or yellow with a bad smell

    See a doctor

    Could be an infection, including an STI.

  • Pain or burning when you ejaculate or pee

    See a doctor

    Often an infection or prostate inflammation. Both are treatable.

  • Discharge when you're not aroused

    See a doctor

    Drip from the penis that isn't urine, semen, or precum is a common STI sign.

Protection

Semen and precum can carry HIV, chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, hepatitis B, and other infections. Many STIs have no symptoms, so you can have one and not know.

  • Put a condom on before any genital contact, not just before you finish.
  • Get tested regularly if you're sexually active, even without symptoms. Many clinics test for free or cheap, and teens can often get tested confidentially.
  • Ask a doctor about the HPV vaccine and, if it fits your situation, PrEP to prevent HIV.

The monthly self-check

Testicular cancer is one of the most common cancers in young men, and it's very treatable when caught early. Checking takes a minute.

  1. Do it in or after a warm shower, when the skin is relaxed.
  2. Hold one testicle at a time and roll it gently between your thumb and fingers.
  3. Feel for hard lumps, swelling, or changes in size or firmness. One being slightly bigger or lower is normal.
  4. The soft tube at the back is the epididymis. That's supposed to be there.
  5. If anything is new, painless, and hard, see a doctor soon. Most lumps turn out to be harmless, but don't wait to find out.

Talking about it

The awkward part is usually just the first sentence. Some ways to start:

To a doctorI've noticed blood in my semen a couple of times. Is that something we should check?
To a partnerI want to use a condom. Let's grab one before we go further.
To a doctorI finish a lot faster than I want to. What are my options?
To a friendIs it just me or did nobody ever explain any of this to us?

Why talk about it at all?

When something's treated as gross or embarrassing, people stop asking questions. They miss signs of an infection, believe myths about pregnancy, or feel broken over things that are normal. Talking about it plainly fixes that.
